**The Problem**

When I send €200 from the Netherlands to Lagos, the naira that lands can differ by ₦12,000 depending on which app I open, how I pay and which day I send. That spread is close to 4% of the transfer, and the sender decides it with very little information about the trade-off.

The World Bank's Remittance Prices Worldwide survey put the average cost of sending $200 to Sub-Saharan Africa at 8.46% in the third quarter of 2025, against a global average of 6.36% and a Sustainable Development Goal of under 3% by 2030. 

Banks charged 14.9% on average in the same quarter, while money transfer operators charged 4.7% and mobile operators 4.6%. 

Can we explain why a bank customer pays three times what a money transfer operator charges, because the World Bank quotes a fixed amount from the sender's side on a given day. It doesn't record whether the money arrived, what the receiver had to do to collect it, whether anyone had to open an account, or what made the sender pick that provider. Providers make choices as well, about which payout partner gets a market and where to hold naira liquidity, and those choices shape the menu senders pick from, though they're rarely written down outside the companies that make them.

Nigeria, the first corridor I will be looking at, received $22.8 billion in personal remittances in 2025 by the World Bank's count, so a single percentage point on that flow is worth more than $200 million a year to the households receiving it. Stablecoins get pitched as the fix. My suspicion, which the study will test, is that the people who can open a stablecoin wallet and cash it out in Lagos are mostly the people who can already open a bank account, which would make stablecoins a cheaper pipe for the banked while leaving the access problem where it is.

The goal is to make information on options transparently available to all players of the chain and drive conversations and recommendations that can universally reduce the cost of cross border remittance across all mediums a customer or provider decides to use.

**Your background (about 250 words)<br />**I've spent more than a decade building payments infrastructure at technology companies in Europe and Africa, and most of that time sat on the provider or banking side of the decisions this study looks at. 

I've managed integrations and relationships with leading PSPs, on deciding which payment methods to switch on in which country, and argued over optimising pricing, FX margin, authorisation rates, and geographic coverage of providers.

I also write at [Fintech is Easy](http://fintechiseasy.com) - my payments research and strategy publication, which allows me to dive deeper into payments topics that I have either previously worked on, or interest me, in such a way that I can explain them to payment practitioners and consumers alike. Alongside this, I co-host [Africa Built,](http://africabuilt.xyz) a podcast on the history of the internet and technology in Africa, and have spoken various payments conferences in Africa and Europe

**What you will do during the Fellowship**

My main question is why consumers, and the institutions serving them, choose the corridors and providers they do, and what it would take to make a route both fast and cheap. I'll study four corridors across Africa, Latin America and Southeast Asia, starting with Nigeria and including one route from the US to Latin America.

For each corridor I'll collect quotes from provider calculators at various price ranges across incumbent money transfer operators, digital-first apps, local champions, bank wires and stablecoin routes, comparing every way the receiver can collect. 

Since quotes only show what's advertised, I'll also make real transfers to check the fee, the FX margin against the mid-market rate, the arrival time and the effort it takes each side to get set up. 

Each corridor guide will trace one transfer, from start to finish, showing who touches the money, where the currency changes and what each step costs where possible. 

I will also explore where Interledger and Open Payments could help, so the guides will be specific about where an open standard closes a gap and where licensing or liquidity is the harder barrier.

**An open invitation**<br />I'd like to hear from:

- product, treasury and FX people at money transfer operators, fintechs, banks and stablecoin on- and off-ramps, especially anyone who has priced a corridor into Africa, Latin America or Southeast Asia
- people who send money home from Europe or the US, and people in Nigeria who receive it
- researchers and community organisers in receiving countries who could run interviews in local languages
- central bank and regulatory staff working on remittance or FX rules
- teams running Interledger or Open Payments in production

I would like to know the reason you stopped using a provider, or how your team decides which payout partner gets a corridor.
