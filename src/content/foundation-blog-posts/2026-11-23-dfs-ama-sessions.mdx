Over two Ask Me Anything sessions in November, the Interledger Foundation team answered questions from organizations thinking about applying to the Digital Financial Services (DFS) Grant. This post turns both conversations into a written Q\&A, so the answers are available to everyone in any format.

Both sessions were recorded and are on the Interledger Foundation YouTube channel:

- Programs AMA with Julaire Hall, Program Officer: **Coming Soon**
- Tech AMA with Max Kurapov, Engineering Manager: [https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5z8YVPjUi2c](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5z8YVPjUi2c)

A quick refresher on the grant. It offers selected organizations up to $350,000 USD over 18 months to integrate Rhyza and Open Payments, build network connections, and test digital financial services with users. Stage 1 applications close on Dec 11, 2026, and the full eligibility and application details are here: [LINK](https://submit.interledger.org/submit/369477/2027-digital-financial-services-grant-expression-of-interest/eligibility)

We have lightly edited and summarized the answers for clarity. The recordings remain the best source for the full conversations.

## Programs AMA with Julaire Hall

Held on November 23, this session focused on who the grant is for, how applications are reviewed, and what the 18 months look like. Julaire Hall is the Program Officer for the DFS Grant at the Interledger Foundation.

### What separates an implementation-ready provider from an early-stage project, and what should the expression of interest show?

This call is about moving from ideas to implementation, so readiness is the main test. We look for a clearly defined payment use case, the technical and operational capacity to deliver it, some level of regulatory readiness, and a realistic path to piloting with test users.

Your expression of interest (EOI) should also explain why interoperability matters to the problem you are solving and how the technology will be integrated. The outcomes you expect should be meaningful to the people you want to serve, not only to your own organization.

### Can organizations that are not yet fully licensed apply?

Yes. If you are not yet regulated, we ask you to explain your pathway to compliance in your jurisdiction. Tell us where you are in that process, what is outstanding, what your timelines look like, and which approvals you can realistically expect during the 18 months and after.

If you are already regulated, you can share your license or simply tell us which license you hold. Honesty about where you stand helps us judge what is achievable within the grant period.

### What does it mean to be a regulated entity or hold a license?

The grant targets financial service providers such as banks, credit unions, savings and credit cooperatives, wallet providers, and mobile money providers. We use whatever your country requires to operate. If your jurisdiction requires a money license for what you do, we expect you to hold it or be on a path to get it.

### Does the Foundation prioritize Colombia and the wider Americas over other regions?

This is the first call where we are prioritizing specific regions, and Colombia and the wider Americas are the focus because of the work the Foundation has done there. That said, we welcome applications from anywhere in the world, especially from organizations with regional or global impact.

For example, an organization based in Africa could apply with a service that supports cross-border transactions with the EU, Canada, or Nigeria. We value projects that strengthen interoperable payment networks and create connections across institutions and markets, and every application is reviewed on technical readiness and organizational capacity.

### Who needs to be the applicant? Can a technology company, nonprofit, or consortium lead?

As the application is set up, the applicant should be the financial service provider, either already regulated or on a credible path to becoming regulated. A nonprofit or consortium can apply, but it needs a lead applicant and must explain the regulatory situation in the same way.

If you are a technology provider working with a regulated partner, say so clearly. A letter of commitment or partnership showing the partner has agreed to take part will strengthen your application.

### Do we need a signed letter from a regulated partner at the EOI stage?

Letters of support are required in phase two, not in the EOI. If you are a technical implementer and plan to work with a regulated entity, including the letter or some other confirmation that they are on board at EOI stage makes your application stronger, but it is not mandatory.

### Can we start with domestic payments, or must we complete a cross-border transaction?

Domestic payments are welcome, and some of our current grantees focus on domestic services. Cross-border use cases are a natural fit for Interledger, so explain how your domestic pilot connects to that goal.

If you cannot reach cross-border within the 18 months, document what you will achieve in that time and include a sustainability plan for what comes after.

### What are the most common mistakes in an EOI?

The biggest one is focusing on the technology and saying too little about the problem. Our mission is digital financial access, so we want to see what problem you are solving, how our technology fits, and why the solution can scale and be sustained.

We also want realistic projects. Avoid proposing more than your team can deliver, name the partnerships you need, and be clear about the regulatory process you are in or have completed.

### What can selected grantees expect during the 18 months?

Funding is up to $350,000, and you can request less if that is what the project needs. Payments are milestone based, and you will submit a timeline and milestone document that lays out what you plan to do month by month.

Support includes monthly check-ins with our program and tech teams, technical guidance from our integration specialists, and opportunities to showcase your work. You can also demo your project and ask for feedback on our community calls, held on the second Wednesday of every month.

### If our banking partner's production approval takes longer than planned, can we start with sandbox testing?

Yes, there is a testing environment. Max Kurapov from the tech team explained that we run a test wallet, which works like a test financial service provider, and you can peer and test transactions between instances while you build your product.

The session did not cover how milestones would be adjusted if production approval is delayed. We recommend asking the team directly in Slack.

### When is the first payment made, and is it an advance or a reimbursement?

It is an upfront payment, not a reimbursement. After you sign the agreement and complete onboarding, you are invited to submit your first invoice and usually receive payment within 30 days.

Subsequent payments are tied to your milestones. After you submit a report, our team reviews it, and once it is approved you are invited to submit the next invoice.

### What minimum evidence of user adoption and interoperability is expected?

There is no minimum for users, transaction volume, or connected institutions. You set the targets in your application, and your progress is measured against them in monthly updates and reports.

For example, if you have 15,000 members but plan to pilot with 100 users, then 100 is the number you are measured against. By the end of the program, we do expect you to have completed live testing with people.

### How many organizations will advance, and how many will be awarded?

The process has two phases. We hope to advance about 10 organizations from the EOI stage to a full proposal, and we plan to award three projects for this call.

## Tech AMA with Max Kurapov

Held on November 20, this session covered how to integrate Rhyza and Open Payments into an existing financial service. Max Kurapov is an Engineering Manager at the Interledger Foundation, and this grant is the team's main chance to work with integrators and connect more people through open source Interledger technology.

### As a new participant, how do we find institutions to connect with, and is there a test network?

There is a test environment. The Foundation runs its own test wallet, and an integrations team operates a range of Open Payments applications for testing. As an integrator, you can work with that team to integrate in the test environment.

For production connections between nodes and institutions, we handle it case by case and can help facilitate introductions, depending on your region. You can also start conversations with other institutions yourself. Since we cannot know your project before you apply, these partnership discussions are best had after a grant is awarded, when we can also see how close you are to other grantees.

### Is the grant targeting a particular region, such as Africa?

The grant prioritizes Colombia and the wider Americas but is not restricted to them. Organizations in Africa and elsewhere can apply if their project meets the eligibility requirements, and strong global or multi-regional projects are considered.

### Can Open Payments support everyday services such as merchant checkout, QR payments, airtime, and bill payments?

Broadly yes. It depends on the Open Payments applications you build, since any application that can make requests to the Open Payments APIs can support different use cases. Examples include marketplaces, utility companies collecting bills, and remittance services that connect a sender and a receiver.

For QR payments, you can encode an incoming payment, which is something to be paid later, in a QR code. A customer scans it and goes through the Open Payments flow to pay. The Open Payments documentation shows how to build these applications.

### How should Rhyza work alongside providers handling currency conversion, liquidity, and settlement, for example to let users receive stablecoins and spend through banks and mobile money in Africa?

The answer depends on your use case and on which institution is running Rhyza. A financial service provider already has user accounts, and Rhyza opens up pathways for Open Payments and Interledger Protocol (ILP) integrations on top of them.

Those payments are separate from how you handle on-ramps, off-ramps, and settlement. Two institutions can send payments to each other over ILP while settling in whatever way suits them. Rhyza makes no assumptions about how you move money across liquidity providers or how users move funds in and out. It creates the bridge for ILP payments and Open Payments functionality, and the rest is your choice.

### Since we are preparing for the 2027 DFS grants, what should we prioritize now to show our infrastructure is ready for Rhyza?

You do not need a working integration at this stage. The most useful step is to understand the flows and APIs by going through the documentation.

A good exercise is to draw a sequence diagram of how a payment would move through your system. It helps you, and it shows reviewers that you have thought about how Rhyza fits into your current setup. Also think about the webhook events you would listen for, the integration steps and admin API, and how you would issue wallet addresses for your user accounts so they become Open Payments enabled.

### What are the core infrastructure prerequisites for integrating Rhyza into an existing stack?

In four loose steps, all covered on the integration page of the Rhyza documentation:

1. Deploy the Rhyza services. You can start in our local playground.
2. Call the admin APIs to set up the resources you need.
3. Write a service that listens to the events Rhyza sends out and connects them to your own ledger.
4. Integrate with the identity provider, which powers the approval screen a customer sees when authorizing an online payment.

### How does Rhyza's architecture improve scalability compared to Rafiki?

The short answer is that Rhyza components scale horizontally. In Rafiki, Open Payments, ILP, and the admin API were packed into a single backend service. Running more instances of that backend did not add performance or let it handle more requests.

With Rhyza, we split the system up architecturally so that each component can scale on its own. The more instances of a service you deploy, the more requests you can handle.

### What technical maturity does the review panel expect?

There is no single standard. Tell us how your team is set up, including your developer and infrastructure teams, and what experience you have running similar systems. Prior experience deploying and connecting services is a good sign.

We expect both large institutions and smaller ones to apply, and the requirements do not set specific rules about team makeup. We are looking at feasibility, so describe your organization and how it would carry out the technical work.

### How do Open Payments and Rhyza's new card authorization flows bridge card rails with Interledger networks?

This is a new development. The Open Payments APIs recently gained a field that supports card authorization within Open Payments flows, and we are at an early stage of experimenting with it.

The longer-term idea is to process card transactions as part of the Open Payments grant authorization flow, bridging in-person merchant payments with Interledger. If you have ideas or experience with card payments, we would like to hear them.

### By the end of the 18 months, what milestones define a successful implementation?

The grant does not set numbers such as a required count of live transactions or peering relationships. The focus is a feasible plan for taking the product into a live environment, including the regulatory and compliance steps that come up in your jurisdiction.

Processing live transactions and peering with other institutions would be a strong outcome. What we care about most is feasibility in practice, not hitting a target such as five peers or 100,000 transactions.

### How many applicants will move to the full proposal stage?

The maximum is $350,000 per awardee. In phase one we hope to shortlist 10 applicants and invite them to submit a full proposal in phase two.
