## The Problem

Many things we see as normal today were born from the push of organized groups: the eight-hour workday, women’s right to vote, even something as everyday as having your own bank account. In Spain, for example, a married woman needed her husband’s permission to open an account until 1975, when a reform driven by a group of jurists removed that “marital licence”. Today this is hard to imagine, and that is exactly why it is worth asking which things we accept as normal now will look just as unfair tomorrow. Access to basic financial services is one of them: when it is missing, the ability to act is limited.

What was true for people is also true for organizations. An association, a cooperative, or a mutual aid network needs an account to receive funds, pay its team, contract services, and report with transparency. When opening or keeping that account is slow, costly, or uncertain, the work becomes harder. In my experience in the financial management of civil society organizations, many funding calls require an account in the organization’s own name, so without one, access to resources can become difficult. This is not just a hypothesis: a 2026 guide published by ICNL, based on consultations in the region, found that receiving or sending funds from abroad is among the situations that most often lead a financial institution to request additional information from an organization.

There is a lot of talk about the financial inclusion of individuals and small businesses, but less about organizations. It is not always clear why these frictions appear: they may come from regulation, from each institution’s practices, or from incentives designed for other kinds of clients. Telling these causes apart is necessary to propose solutions that truly work.

## **Your background**

I come to this topic from both sides of the counter. I worked for more than a decade in banking, in Central America and Spain, from direct customer service to running a branch, with experience in trade finance, credit analysis, and anti-money-laundering. I am familiar with how a financial institution assesses a client and the compliance rules it works under.

Later, as an independent consultant, I worked in the financial and administrative management of civil society organizations. There I saw the other side of the counter: what it means to depend on these processes to operate. Between these two experiences, the question that guides this project took shape: why financial access, which for a company is usually a routine step, can be much harder for a civil society organization. To this dual perspective I bring data analysis and actor mapping tools, which I want to use to organize and compare information from several countries.

## **What will you do during the Fellowship?**

- Main question: how the financial access difficulties that civil society organizations face take shape, what produces them, and under what conditions open payment standards, such as Open Payments and the Interledger Protocol, could reduce some of them.
- Approach: a comparative analysis of the legal framework, with local legal advice, together with an exploratory survey and interviews with organizations, payment service providers, regulators, and donors, plus fieldwork in the region.
- What I will produce: research instruments and an actor mapping; an anonymized interview dataset; a friction map and comparative findings; and, at the end, a technical paper with practical recommendations, including where open standards can help and where they cannot.
- Where to follow the progress: in the community calls and in my monthly posts on the Community Forum, starting in December.

The conclusions will be entirely my own, the study does not evaluate any institution, and participation is handled confidentially.
